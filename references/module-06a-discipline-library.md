### 5. Discipline-specific framework and methodology library

Use this as a discovery index, not a mandatory checklist. Validate suitability and adapt to the actual assignment.

**A. Product design, UX/UI, service design and HCI**

Typical purpose: demonstrate how research, user needs and product constraints were translated into a usable experience and designed interfaces.

Potential process/frameworks:
- Design Thinking: Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. Adapt stages to what genuinely occurred.
- Double Diamond: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver; use to explain divergence and convergence.
- Human-Centred Design (HCD) and ISO 9241-210 principles where applicable.
- Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) for understanding progress users seek to make.
- Lean UX for hypothesis-driven, collaborative design.
- Service Design for frontstage, backstage, people, processes and touchpoints.
- Design Sprint where the project genuinely used a time-boxed sprint.
- Participatory/co-design approaches where stakeholders actively shaped solutions.
- Accessibility and inclusive-design principles where relevant.
- Usability heuristics, cognitive walkthroughs and task-based usability evaluation where actually applied.

Typical evidence and artefacts:
- Research plan, interview/survey/observation evidence, desk research and competitive review.
- Research synthesis, affinity mapping, themes, insight statements and opportunity areas.
- Stakeholder maps, user archetypes/personas, empathy maps and jobs-to-be-done.
- Current-state and future-state journey maps, experience maps and service blueprints.
- Problem statements, How Might We questions, design principles and opportunity mapping.
- Feature definition, prioritisation rationale, requirements and hypothesis mapping.
- Information architecture, content hierarchy, sitemap, taxonomy and navigation model.
- User/task flows, edge cases, decision trees and interaction flows.
- Sketches, low/mid/high-fidelity wireframes, iterations and design rationale.
- High-fidelity UI screens, responsive states, design systems, components and interaction patterns.
- Clickable prototypes, usability findings, accessibility checks and developer handoff specifications.

Expected narrative logic:
Research context → evidence and synthesis → user needs and problem definition → design opportunities → product/experience strategy → IA and task architecture → user journeys and flows → wireframes and iterations → high-fidelity interfaces → prototype and evaluation → handoff, reflection and measured outcomes where available.

**Critical product-design rule:** The case must show the translation from evidence to design. Do not merely display polished screens or describe features. For every major design decision, connect:
Research evidence or observed need → synthesised insight → design principle/requirement → product or interaction decision → relevant flow → actual interface screen → validation or limitation.

When evidence is incomplete, distinguish a verified research finding from the designer's interpretation, assumption or design hypothesis.

Do not imply that the designer performed software engineering, backend implementation, deployment, product analytics or production QA unless evidence confirms it. Clearly distinguish design ownership, design leadership, research collaboration, prototyping, developer handoff and implementation by others.

**B. Product management and product strategy**

Typical purpose: explain how a product opportunity was identified, prioritised, shaped, launched or managed.

Potential approaches: product discovery; Opportunity Solution Tree; JTBD; Lean Startup Build-Measure-Learn; hypothesis-driven development; RICE or other prioritisation models; product lifecycle and portfolio analysis; North Star metric and outcome-based roadmaps.

Typical evidence: customer/problem discovery, market and competitor analysis, opportunity sizing, product requirements, prioritisation decisions, roadmap trade-offs, experiments, adoption and product metrics.

Narrative logic: context → opportunity/problem → evidence → strategic choices → prioritisation → product definition → delivery/experimentation → adoption/results → learning. Do not turn a design-only contribution into product ownership or delivery ownership.

**C. Business strategy, management and consulting**

Typical purpose: diagnose an organisational or market problem, assess strategic options and explain decisions or recommendations.

Potential approaches: SWOT/TOWS, PESTLE, Porter's Five Forces, value-chain analysis, business model canvas, capability analysis, issue trees, hypothesis-driven consulting, root-cause analysis, theory of change and scenario analysis.

Typical evidence: financial and operational data, market evidence, stakeholder interviews, process records, benchmarks, organisational capabilities, risks and strategic constraints.

Narrative logic: executive context → business challenge → diagnostic evidence → root causes → strategic alternatives → recommendation → implementation plan (if applicable) → outcomes or expected value → risks and lessons.

**D. Software engineering, systems architecture and technical implementation**

Typical purpose: explain a technical problem, architecture, engineering decisions, build process, integration, reliability or performance.

Potential approaches: requirements engineering; systems engineering V-model; architecture trade-off analysis; ADRs (architecture decision records); threat modelling; DevOps/CI-CD; Agile delivery; test strategy; SRE and reliability practices; incident/postmortem analysis.

Typical evidence: requirements, architecture diagrams, code/repository records, API contracts, test results, performance benchmarks, security assessments, deployment records, incident logs and operational metrics.

Narrative logic: technical context → requirements/problem → constraints → architecture/options → engineering decisions → implementation → verification/testing → deployment/operation → measured results and trade-offs.

Do not use this structure for a design prototype that was only handed to developers. Identify the actual technical owner and stage.

**E. AI, data science and automation**

Typical purpose: demonstrate how an AI/data opportunity was defined, evaluated, designed, built, governed or deployed.

Potential approaches: CRISP-DM; data lifecycle and governance; problem framing and value assessment; human-in-the-loop design; model evaluation; responsible AI risk assessment; NIST AI Risk Management Framework; automation process analysis; workflow redesign.

Typical evidence: problem definition, data provenance and quality, baseline, model/system selection, evaluation metrics, error analysis, human oversight, privacy/security controls, deployment conditions, monitoring and user impact.

Narrative logic: use case → suitability and feasibility → data/workflow context → solution architecture or experience design → evaluation → human oversight and risk controls → deployment status → performance and limitations.

Distinguish an AI-enabled concept, AI-assisted prototype, tested model, integrated product and production AI system. Do not claim model training, autonomous operation, accuracy, cost savings or production deployment without evidence.

**F. Engineering, construction, infrastructure and built environment**

Potential approaches: systems engineering; requirements traceability; design reviews; risk assessment; HAZID/HAZOP where relevant; lifecycle assessment; constructability analysis; value engineering; project controls; BIM workflows; failure-mode analysis; sustainability assessment.

Typical evidence: specifications, drawings, site records, calculations, inspection reports, risk registers, schedule/cost data, compliance records, stakeholder requirements and commissioning results.

Narrative logic: project context → technical/social requirements → constraints and risks → options/design → engineering decisions → delivery/construction → inspection/commissioning → operational performance and lessons.

Use the standards and jurisdiction relevant to the actual project. Do not imply safety certification or engineering approval without proof.

**G. Healthcare, clinical and public health**

Potential approaches: clinical reasoning and differential diagnosis; evidence-based practice; PICO; clinical audit; quality improvement (PDSA); RE-AIM; implementation science; patient-safety analysis; epidemiological study designs.

Typical evidence: de-identified clinical records, validated measures, guidelines, audit data, patient outcomes, consent and ethics approvals where applicable.

Narrative logic: presentation/context → assessment → evidence and differential → intervention/decision → implementation → follow-up/outcomes → limitations and learning.

Protect patient privacy and distinguish educational case material from clinical advice. Use appropriate professional and ethical standards.

**H. Public policy, government, community development and social impact**

Potential approaches: theory of change; logic model; stakeholder/power mapping; policy-cycle analysis; institutional analysis; participatory action research; contribution analysis; outcome harvesting; realist evaluation; social return on investment where data supports it.

Typical evidence: policy documents, community consultation, administrative data, stakeholder perspectives, baseline/context indicators, programme records and evaluation findings.

Narrative logic: public/community context → affected groups and needs → problem/system diagnosis → intervention design → participation and implementation → outputs/outcomes → equity, unintended effects and lessons.

Do not equate consultation activity with representative participation or demonstrated social impact.

**I. Marketing, brand, communications and growth**

Potential approaches: STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning); customer journey; marketing funnel; AIDA where suitable; brand positioning; messaging hierarchy; campaign planning; channel analysis; experimentation and attribution models.

Typical evidence: audience research, brand audits, campaign assets, channel analytics, conversion data, customer feedback, media spend and attribution limitations.

Narrative logic: market/audience context → communication challenge → insight/positioning → strategy and creative direction → channel/campaign execution → measurement → results and learning.

**J. Operations, process improvement and supply chain**

Potential approaches: Lean, Six Sigma/DMAIC, value-stream mapping, process mapping, Theory of Constraints, root-cause analysis, FMEA, service blueprinting and continuous improvement.

Typical evidence: process maps, cycle times, error rates, service levels, capacity, costs, waste, quality data and operational observations.

Narrative logic: operating context → process problem → baseline and diagnosis → root causes → redesigned process → pilot/implementation → measured performance → control and learning.

**K. Education, training and learning design**

Potential approaches: ADDIE, backward design, constructive alignment, Bloom's taxonomy, Kirkpatrick evaluation and experiential learning.

Typical evidence: learner needs, learning objectives, curriculum, assessment design, participation, learner work, evaluation and measured learning outcomes.

Narrative logic: learner/context → learning gap → objectives → intervention design → delivery → assessment/evaluation → learning evidence → iteration.

**L. Research, science and academic inquiry**

Potential approaches: research question and hypothesis development; qualitative, quantitative or mixed-methods design; case-study methodology; systematic/scoping review; grounded theory; phenomenology; ethnography; experimental/quasi-experimental design; statistical or thematic analysis.

Typical evidence: protocol, sampling, instruments, datasets, ethics, analysis, reproducibility, limitations and references.

Narrative logic: abstract → introduction/problem → literature/context → research question → methodology → findings → discussion → limitations → conclusion and references. Follow the required discipline and publication conventions.

**M. Finance, investment and economics**

Potential approaches: financial statement analysis; unit economics; DCF and valuation; scenario/sensitivity analysis; cost-benefit analysis; portfolio/risk analysis; econometric or policy evaluation methods.

Typical evidence: financial statements, assumptions, market data, cash flows, rates, timeframes, comparable entities and model limitations.

Narrative logic: financial context → question → data and assumptions → analytical model → scenarios and sensitivity → findings → risks and implications.

**N. Failure, crisis, incident and recovery**

Potential approaches: incident timeline; root-cause analysis; Five Whys; fishbone/Ishikawa; fault-tree analysis; FMEA; Swiss-cheese model; blameless postmortem; resilience engineering.

Typical evidence: contemporaneous records, logs, timelines, witness accounts, system conditions, decisions, impact and recovery records.

Narrative logic: context → incident → timeline → impact → contributing factors and root causes → response/recovery → corrective actions → residual risk and lessons.

Avoid hindsight bias and unsupported blame.

**O. Entrepreneurship, startup and venture development**

Potential approaches: Lean Startup; Business Model Canvas; Value Proposition Canvas; customer discovery; JTBD; validation experiments; feasibility analysis; unit economics; venture milestones and stage-gate decisions.

Typical evidence: customer discovery, market research, experiments, prototypes, traction, financial assumptions, channel tests and founder decisions.

Narrative logic: opportunity → customer/problem evidence → proposition → business model → experiments and pivots → product/market development → traction or unresolved risks → lessons.

Do not portray a concept or prototype as a validated business.
