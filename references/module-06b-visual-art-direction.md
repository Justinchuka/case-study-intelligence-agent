## MODULE 06B --- VISUAL ART DIRECTION, NARRATIVE DESIGN & PORTFOLIO PRODUCTION INTELLIGENCE

### 1. Purpose

Turn the approved case-study architecture into a visually exceptional, coherent and persuasive visual experience. This module applies when the requested deliverable is a Behance project, design portfolio, product/UX case study, visual report, presentation or other artifact where visual communication is central.

Do not treat visual production as decorating completed copy. Narrative, evidence, copy, diagrams, imagery, interface artefacts and composition must be designed together. The visual experience is part of the argument.

### 2. Governing art-direction principle

**Aesthetic excellence through intentional restraint.**

The goal is not maximum graphics, effects, mockups, illustrations, 3D objects or visual complexity. The goal is the highest possible level of visual quality through deliberate judgement. Understand the project, subject, audience, purpose, content and narrative before choosing a visual language.

Use the amount and type of visual treatment the work deserves:
- Add visual richness when it clarifies the subject, creates meaningful atmosphere, improves comprehension or makes the work more compelling.
- Use restraint when the evidence, interface, diagram or typography should carry the attention.
- Use whitespace as an active compositional tool, not as unused space that must be filled.
- Remove decorative elements that do not improve meaning, hierarchy, atmosphere or composition.
- Add elements when they materially strengthen communication, spatial depth, visual balance or narrative.

The standard is not more design. It is better design. Every frame must demonstrate aesthetic judgement, including deliberately minimal frames.

### 3. Adaptive visual-direction diagnosis

Before designing, establish a project-specific art direction. Consider:
- Case-study type and dominant discipline.
- Product, service, subject matter and industry context.
- Audience and the professional qualities the case must demonstrate.
- Narrative tone: analytical, human, technical, editorial, exploratory, institutional, expressive or hybrid.
- Actual artefacts available: research, photography, product screens, diagrams, data, prototypes, documents, physical objects or environments.
- Brand identity and existing design language.
- Platform, viewing context and output dimensions.
- Content density, evidence complexity and visual opportunities.

Then define a concise creative direction covering:
1. Visual concept and rationale.
2. Overall aesthetic character and tone.
3. Palette and colour roles, respecting authentic brand assets where available.
4. Typography system and hierarchy.
5. Grid, margins, spacing scale and alignment rules.
6. Background and surface treatments.
7. Image, illustration, diagram and mockup language.
8. Depth, lighting, shadow, texture and dimensionality rules.
9. Composition principles and layout variation.
10. Visual motifs or recurring elements that create continuity.

Do not impose one universal aesthetic on all case studies. A built-environment product may call for architectural, spatial or material references; a fintech product may need a different visual language; a healthcare service may need a different balance of clarity, trust and human context. These are possibilities, not stereotypes. Derive the direction from the actual project.

### 4. Visual treatment selection intelligence

Choose visual treatments by purpose, not by trend or novelty. For each proposed visual element, ask: What does it communicate? What does it help the viewer understand? Does it improve the composition? Is it authentic, illustrative or conceptual? Is it worth the attention it consumes?

Available treatments include, where suitable:
- Product UI presented directly, at readable scale.
- Desktop, laptop, browser and monitor mockups.
- Smartphone and tablet mockups, including multi-device compositions.
- Perspective, angled, floating, layered or overlapping screen compositions.
- Close-up crops and annotated interface details.
- Before/after and comparative layouts.
- 2D illustrations, editorial imagery, diagrams and infographics.
- 3D objects, architectural forms, spatial scenes, isometric illustrations and dimensional product compositions.
- Photography, environmental context, material studies and subject-specific imagery.
- Subtle grain, paper, architectural, grid, topographic or other restrained textures where contextually appropriate.
- Gradients, tonal fields, shadows, masks, transparency and depth treatments.
- Typographic compositions, section markers, numbers and editorial devices.

Select only what supports the story. A phone mockup is useful when it demonstrates a mobile experience; it is not automatically useful for a desktop workflow. A 3D object is useful when it adds conceptual or contextual meaning; it is not a compulsory decoration. A diagram is useful when it clarifies a relationship or process; it should not restate a simple sentence as unnecessary visual noise.

Never allow mockups, device frames, 3D art or decorative imagery to obscure, shrink or compete with the actual work. In product-design cases, the interface must remain legible and receive appropriate visual prominence.

### 5. Mockup and interface presentation standards

When using device or product mockups:
- Match device type to the actual experience and platform.
- Use credible proportions, perspective, bezels, browser chrome and shadows.
- Place real project screens in mockups; do not invent interface states to fill a composition.
- Preserve accurate screen content and avoid distorted, illegible or misleading UI.
- Vary scale and arrangement intentionally: hero device, paired devices, screen sequence, detail crop or contextual scene.
- Use consistent lighting, perspective and material language within a composition.
- Use device mockups to establish context or hierarchy, not as a substitute for showing the UI clearly.
- Provide direct, flat screen views when precision, annotation or comparison matters.
- Avoid repetitive mockup arrangements across consecutive frames.

If authentic screens are unavailable, do not fabricate them as project evidence. Ask for the missing assets when essential, or use clearly labelled conceptual placeholders or wireframe representations if the user authorises that approach.

### 6. Visual storytelling and frame-to-frame continuity

Treat the complete case study as one continuous narrative experience, not a set of independent slides or disconnected sections.

Create a narrative spine before designing individual frames. For each frame or section, define:
- Its role in the story.
- The question it answers.
- The key message or claim.
- The evidence or artefact that supports it.
- The visual device that makes the message understandable.
- What the viewer should understand or feel after seeing it.
- The question, tension or idea that leads into the next frame.

Every frame must inherit context from what came before and prepare the viewer for what follows. Use meaningful transitions: a problem introduced in one frame is investigated in the next; research evidence is synthesised into insights; insights lead to opportunities; opportunities shape product structure and flows; flows are expressed through wireframes and interfaces; final screens are connected back to the original needs. Do not jump between topics without explaining the relationship.

Use continuity devices selectively: recurring labels, section numbers, visual motifs, colour cues, repeated diagram language, connecting lines, progressive diagrams, consistent image treatments or a continuing spatial composition. Continuity must not make every frame look identical. Preserve a shared visual system while varying composition to maintain rhythm and interest.

Use pacing deliberately. Alternate dense evidence sections with breathing room, detailed diagrams with simple statements, close-up UI with complete product views, and analytical frames with visual reveals. Do not make every frame a hero frame. Do not make every frame a text-heavy explanation.

### 7. Composition, layout and visual hierarchy

Use professional editorial and portfolio design principles:
- Establish a consistent grid, safe margins, alignment logic and spacing scale.
- Create a clear hierarchy among headline, supporting copy, labels, annotations, evidence and imagery.
- Use intentional asymmetry, balance, scale, cropping, negative space and visual tension.
- Vary layouts according to content: full-bleed, editorial columns, asymmetric image/text, split-screen, large typography, evidence boards, diagrams, interface galleries, close-up details and restrained closing compositions.
- Avoid repeating the same card grid, title-plus-bullets layout or device mockup formula on every frame.
- Keep body text readable at the intended Behance viewing size. Do not shrink text to force excessive copy into a frame.
- Maintain consistent typography, spacing, colour roles, corner radii, stroke weights and image treatment.
- Ensure visual weight is balanced; do not leave accidental gaps or overcrowd important content.
- Use contrast and visual emphasis to direct attention, not to decorate indiscriminately.

Composition variety is desirable; design-system consistency is mandatory. Do not confuse consistency with monotonous repetition.

### 8. Backgrounds, materials, texture and effects

Use backgrounds and effects as supporting atmosphere. Choose surface treatments that suit the project and protect legibility. Subtle texture, grain, paper, architectural material, tonal gradients, grid patterns, soft shadows, light, depth and transparency may be used when they add refinement or contextual meaning.

Avoid heavy texture behind dense text, excessive blur, gratuitous glassmorphism, random gradients, loud patterns, ornamental borders, unnecessary glow, overdone shadows and effects that make the work look dated or template-driven. Effects must be consistent and purposeful. Do not apply every effect simply because the design tool supports it.

### 9. Visual proof and evidence integrity

Every visual must have a clear status:
- Authentic project artefact.
- User-supplied image or screenshot.
- Verified external reference.
- Faithful reconstruction from documented evidence.
- Newly created explanatory diagram based on verified information.
- Conceptual illustration or art direction.
- Generated mockup or illustrative asset.
- Hypothetical or sample content.

Never present conceptual, generated, reconstructed or sample material as a real historical project artefact, user research output, product screen, prototype, result or deployment record. Label illustrative content where a reasonable viewer could mistake it for evidence.

Do not fabricate research boards, sticky notes, interview quotes, personas, analytics, dashboards, maps, product screens, stakeholder feedback or usability results. A visual can be aesthetically excellent and still be evidentially false; visual polish never overrides integrity.

Use real project assets whenever possible. External assets must be appropriately licensed or otherwise permitted. Preserve source attribution where required. Do not use generic stock imagery when authentic project material or a more relevant custom visual would communicate better.

### 10. Discipline-specific visual language

Adapt visual communication to the type of work:
- **Product/UX/UI:** Research evidence, synthesis, journeys, IA, flows, wireframes, actual screens, interaction states, prototypes, design systems and handoff. Use mockups and UI galleries to make the designed experience tangible.
- **Architecture/built environment:** Spatial diagrams, plans, sections, maps, material references, contextual imagery, models and carefully selected 3D views, only where they represent actual or clearly conceptual work.
- **Business/strategy:** Decision trees, issue structures, strategic options, operating models, evidence charts and concise executive visualisations.
- **Engineering/technical:** Accurate system diagrams, schematics, architecture views, process flows, technical illustrations and clearly labelled measurements.
- **Research/academic:** Method diagrams, evidence tables, study designs, data visualisations and precise figure captions.
- **AI/data:** Workflow diagrams, human/system interaction, data provenance, model evaluation and oversight visualisations. Do not imply model capability that is not evidenced.
- **Service/public-sector/community:** Stakeholder relationships, service journeys, ecosystem maps, service blueprints, participation pathways and accessible information design.

This is a guidance index, not a fixed visual template. Hybrid cases should combine visual languages only where they genuinely support the case.

### 11. Visual narrative and content integration

Do not write a long block of copy and then force it into a layout. Design the communication unit as a whole:
- A headline makes the section's argument.
- A short explanation supplies necessary context.
- An artefact or visual demonstrates the claim.
- An annotation directs attention to the important detail.
- A caption explains what the viewer should learn from the artefact.
- A transition connects the point to the next stage.

Use concise, specific writing, but do not remove necessary reasoning merely to create empty minimalist frames. Preserve the evidence trail and the logic behind decisions. The viewer should understand not only what was designed, but why it was designed that way and how the work progressed.

### 12. Tool, Figma and plugin intelligence

When the user requests a Figma deliverable or Figma AI prompt, tailor production instructions to the actual Figma environment and available capabilities. Use Auto Layout, components, variants, styles or variables, grids, masks, vector tools, effects, image fills and reusable patterns where they improve consistency, editability or production quality.

Use plugins, community resources, mockup libraries, illustration libraries, image tools or other integrations only when they are genuinely available, permitted and useful. Discover or verify availability rather than inventing plugin names, claiming access or assuming that an AI agent can operate a plugin autonomously. Do not recommend plugins as a substitute for design judgement.

If the requested AI tool has limitations, distinguish what it can generate directly from what requires manual refinement, imported assets, plugins or another tool. Do not promise autonomous operations that the platform cannot perform. If a prompt has a strict character limit, preserve the full creative intent in a supporting specification or staged workflow rather than deleting essential direction to fit the limit.

For a Figma AI prompt, follow `references/figma-prompt-protocol.md`. It governs when the prompt is offered, the character limit, and its separation from the finished deliverables.

### 13. Production workflow

For visual case-study production, follow this sequence, adapting to the tool and scope:
1. Confirm the case classification, audience, narrative and evidence status.
2. Build the narrative spine and section transitions.
3. Audit available brand assets, screenshots, diagrams, photography and project artefacts.
4. Define the creative direction and visual system.
5. Create a visual storyboard or frame plan showing message, evidence, layout concept and transition for each frame.
6. Establish the master grid, typography, spacing, palette, surface treatments and reusable components.
7. Design the opening and a representative interior frame to test the art direction before extending it.
8. Produce all frames with intentional composition variation and continuous storytelling.
9. Add mockups, diagrams, illustrations, 3D elements and textures only where they improve the result.
10. Review the full sequence at presentation scale and as one continuous scroll or sequence.
11. Refine weak frames, transitions, image positioning, spacing, hierarchy, legibility and consistency.
12. Verify that every visual is accurate, properly labelled and technically usable.

Do not assume a fixed frame count. Determine the length from the story, platform and user requirements. If the user specifies a frame count, work within it while protecting narrative completeness.

### 14. Visual quality review and release criteria

Review the complete work from the perspective of an experienced portfolio art director, editorial designer, discipline specialist and first-time viewer. Evaluate:
- Narrative continuity: Does each frame lead naturally to the next?
- Story clarity: Can the viewer explain what happened, why it mattered and how the work developed?
- Evidence visibility: Are the actual artefacts and design decisions visible and legible?
- Art direction: Does the visual language suit this specific project?
- Aesthetic quality: Are typography, composition, spacing, image treatment, surfaces and hierarchy professionally resolved?
- Intentional restraint: Is every graphic element justified? Is anything excessive, generic or distracting?
- Mockup quality: Are devices and interface presentations credible, varied and useful?
- Visual rhythm: Does the sequence have a considered pace and appropriate variation?
- Cohesion: Does it feel like one publication or visual experience rather than disconnected slides?
- Accuracy: Are all screens, claims, images and diagrams authentic or correctly labelled?
- Platform readiness: Is the content readable and visually effective at the intended display size?

If any major area is weak, revise before release. Do not declare a case study finished merely because all frames exist. Completion requires both narrative integrity and high visual craft.
