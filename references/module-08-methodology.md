## MODULE 08 --- ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK & METHODOLOGY INTELLIGENCE

### 1. Purpose

Select, justify and apply research designs, methods, analytical
frameworks and theoretical or conceptual approaches appropriate to the
case.

### 2. Distinctions

Do not confuse: - Methodology: overall logic and justification of
inquiry. - Method: a specific procedure for collecting or analysing
information. - Research design: the structure of the investigation. -
Analytical framework: an organising structure for interpreting
evidence. - Theoretical framework: established theory used to explain or
interpret. - Conceptual framework: a model of relevant concepts and
relationships. - Evaluation framework: criteria and structure for
assessing performance or outcomes. - Diagnostic tool: a procedure for
identifying causes or conditions. - Analytical technique: a specific
reasoning or analysis procedure. - Measurement instrument: a tool for
measuring a variable or construct.

### 3. Methodological families

Consider: - Qualitative. - Quantitative. - Mixed methods. - Experimental
and intervention-based. - Applied and professional. - Theoretical and
conceptual. - Historical and archival. - Comparative and evaluative.

### 4. Research design selection

Consider: - Single-case or multiple-case. - Holistic or embedded. -
Historical or contemporary. - Retrospective, prospective or
longitudinal. - Comparative. - Exploratory, descriptive, explanatory or
evaluative. - Intervention or non-intervention. - Primary or secondary
research. - Unit of analysis and case boundaries.

### 5. Framework discovery

Use Module 06A's discipline-specific library as a starting index, then discover and verify frameworks relevant to the actual assignment across business, management, product/UX/service design, engineering, AI/data, operations, evaluation, public policy, economics, healthcare, education, sustainability, risk and systems.

First classify the case and its purpose. Then distinguish framework, methodology, method, model, tool, technique and artefact. Verify names, origins and applicability when necessary. Select only frameworks that fit the question, lifecycle stage and evidence. Do not framework-stack or retrofit a formal method that was not actually used.

### 6. Selection criteria

Evaluate: - Fit with the research question. - Fit with the discipline. -
Evidence compatibility. - Explanatory or evaluative value. - Scope and
complexity. - Data requirements. - Limitations. - Audience expectations.

Do not select a fashionable framework merely because it is familiar.

### 7. Multiple frameworks

Use multiple frameworks only when they add distinct value. Explain how
they complement each other. Avoid framework stacking and duplicated
analysis.

### 8. Application protocol

For each selected framework: 1. Explain why it fits. 2. Define its
relevant components. 3. Map evidence to components. 4. Identify gaps. 5.
Interpret findings. 6. Identify limitations. 7. Connect findings to the
central question.

Do not merely name-drop frameworks.

### 9. Evidence-method compatibility

Check whether the evidence can support the selected method. Do not claim
statistical inference from an inadequate sample, causal attribution from
a simple before-and-after account, or generalisability from a single
case without qualification.

### 10. Causal reasoning

Consider: - Temporal sequence. - Plausible mechanisms. - Baseline and
comparison. - Confounding variables. - Selection effects. - Alternative
explanations. - Measurement quality. - Counterfactual limitations.

Use the strongest defensible causal language, not the strongest
rhetorically attractive language.

### 11. Methodology justification

Explain why the approach is suitable and what it cannot establish. Seek
user approval when methodology creates consequential trade-offs or
requires access, consent or resources.
